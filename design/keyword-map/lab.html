      The sense of each word

      A word on its own gets its most common sense, which may not be the one the text means. So each keyword is looked up in WordNet and given the sense it has in these sources; the meaning map embeds “word: definition”. Highlighted rows differ from the dictionary’s first sense. Senses were chosen by Claude after reading every sentence each word appears in; in the app this would be one Claude call. A small model alone picked wrongly too often to trust.

      What we're actually deriving

      In this text shows how the writing is organized: which ideas are talked about in the same breath. That's the author's framing, not a fact about the world.

      In meaning is the baseline: how the words relate in everyday English, before anyone wrote this text.

      The gap is the finding. Here the text ties the old channel to money (channel + million, million + concrete, million + council) and the river to people's homes (river + homes). It keeps construction apart from concrete and from homes, and the budget apart from the vote: the new build, the old channel and residents' homes are discussed in different places, and the vote isn't argued in budget terms. A reader might sense this without noticing it.

      Some gaps are noise. "last" is in the keyword list only because of phrases like "last century" and "the last road works", so pairs such as million + last mean little. Better keyword filtering in Trends would drop it.

      Read the gaps with care. A gap is only real if the meaning side reads each word the way the text does. With bare words, channel + concrete ranked #187 of 190 in meaning; given the sense "a passage for water", it moves to #69, and river + channel to #4. channel + million stays far apart (#162) either way, so that link really is the text's framing. Switch "Meaning of each word" to compare.

      Where it gets strong: comparing sources. If one source pairs cost with flood and another pairs cost with construction, they frame the same story differently. On one short article it's a sketch; treat it as a prompt to go and read, not a conclusion.

      The visual variables

      Each one can carry a different piece of information. The rule of thumb: position, and the background regions drawn from it, show the groups. Size shows how common a word is. Color names the group. Avoid making two of them say the same thing, or the map gets louder without saying more.
